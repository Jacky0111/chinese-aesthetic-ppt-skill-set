# Palace Caisson: Historical Sources and Design Interpretation

## Verified facts

- The Palace Museum describes a caisson as a raised, domed ceiling feature at the center of an interior ceiling, commonly used in important halls. Its glossary describes forms built up from square, octagonal, and circular wells.
- The caisson is placed at the center of the ceiling above the throne in the Hall of Supreme Harmony; its center bears a coiled dragon holding a pearl. The Palace Museum's youth education page describes the hall's three-tier caisson as the highest-ranking palace caisson.
- A Palace Museum conservation study of specific painted surfaces in Donghua Gate reports pigments including stone blue, stone green, and cinnabar, and analyzes paint layers, ground materials, and traces of repairs from different periods. The study notes that some layer interpretations require comparison with further cases.
- The Donghua Gate samples are specific architectural paintings and ceiling samples. They are not a direct pigment analysis of the Hall of Supreme Harmony caisson or all palace caissons.

## Modern design interpretation

The requested palette uses gold, stone blue, and stone green. Palace Museum research on specific architectural paintings provides a nearby historical case for stone blue and stone green; gold serves as a design accent that evokes courtly craft, not a universal historical caisson color proportion. Layered structure becomes information hierarchy and spatial depth. Pigment buildup, subtle wear, and mural-like texture are modern visual language, not reconstructions of one historical object. The visual examples are not historical evidence.

Some traditional texts and museum education materials explain the caisson as symbolizing water and as an apotropaic device against fire. Treat this as a traditional belief, not as actual fire-protection performance. If a slide mentions it, label it as a traditional interpretation.
